# Weekly App Ideas — October 11, 2026

**Idea 1: HandloadHQ**

**Problem:** Competitive shooters and handloaders track their load development — powder charges, bullet weights, seating depths, range results — in paper notebooks or janky spreadsheets. Existing apps (Load Data, ReloadersCafe) are mobile-only with 2012-era UX, can't cleanly handle multiple calibers or cross-reference published max charges for safety warnings, and make it impossible to clone and adapt a proven load for a new rifle. Critical safety data lives in dead notebooks that can't be searched, compared, or audited. When a shooter works up a new load across six range sessions, reconstructing what worked and why is genuinely painful.

**Target buyer:** Precision rifle shooters and competitive pistol competitors (PRS, USPSA, 3-gun) who handload — specifically people spending $500+/year on components and who track group sizes, velocities, and chrono data. Not the guy who loads 9mm to save money on range days.

**Monetization:** One-time desktop license at $49. A niche this tight doesn't want subscriptions — they want to own the tool. Realistic early target: 500 customers = $24,500, which is meaningful at side-project pace. A later "club license" tier ($149 for a shooting club to share load libraries) extends it without adding headcount.

**MVP scope:** Desktop app (Tauri or Electron wrapping Python logic). Create and store load recipes (caliber, brass, primer, powder charge, bullet, COAL). Log range sessions against a recipe (group sizes, MV, weather notes). Flag if a charge exceeds published Hodgdon/SAAMI max for that powder/caliber combination. Export a session report to PDF. Does NOT yet do: inventory tracking, component shopping lists, barcode scanning, mobile sync, or a community load database.

**Distribution:** r/reloading (530K members), r/longrange, r/competitionshooting. Forum-first: Sniper's Hide and Brian Enos Forum are where the actual buyers live and have long memories for good tools. A short YouTube walkthrough posted to precision rifle channels. Offer 50 free beta licenses to forum regulars in exchange for feedback — that community will do the word-of-mouth work.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** The TAM looks tiny to a VC but is perfectly sized for a solo dev. Existing apps haven't meaningfully updated in years because no one with taste is building for this niche. A developer who actually shoots understands why a 0.3gr charge difference matters and can build the UX around that — a product manager at a big company would miss every nuance. The community rewards builders who participate authentically.

**Biggest risk:** The handloader demographic skews older and has strong inertia toward paper notebooks. The harder sell isn't price — it's convincing someone their 30-year-old workflow needs replacing.

**Idea 2: PitchKit**

**Problem:** Indie developers doing their own PR before a Steam launch build elaborate spreadsheets to track journalists, YouTubers, and Twitch streamers — who got a review key, who responded, who published, when embargoes lift, which coverage drove wishlists. After launch, that spreadsheet is dead. The next game starts from scratch. Generic CRMs (HubSpot, Notion) have no concept of Steam keys, embargo dates, content tier logic, or "did this streamer's video move the needle." The workflow of indie game PR is specific enough that every dev reinvents it, badly, under launch-week stress.

**Target buyer:** Solo and two-person indie studios self-publishing on Steam in the $10–$25 price range who are doing their own outreach — specifically developers who've shipped at least one game and already know this pain point exists. Not first-timers who don't know what they don't know yet.

**Monetization:** SaaS at $15/month or $99/year, framed as a per-launch tool you activate 60–90 days before release. A $199 lifetime deal on launch for early adopters. Target: 300 paying users = $45K ARR. Indie developers actively buy productivity tools — this community spends money on things that make their work less miserable.

**MVP scope:** Web app (React frontend, Python/FastAPI backend). Contact database with tags (journalist, streamer, Discord mod, content tier). Outreach log (date contacted, template used, response status). Steam key field per contact (you generate keys on Steam, paste them here). Embargo date per contact with a reminder email. Coverage log (link, publish date, estimated reach). Simple dashboard: keys issued vs. coverage received. Does NOT yet do: email sending, automated follow-up sequences, key generation, a pre-loaded media contact database, or analytics integrations with Steam itself.

**Distribution:** r/gamedev, r/indiegaming, the GDC Vault community, and the dozens of "indie dev" Discord servers. Write a public teardown post: "Here's the exact spreadsheet I used to track my indie launch — and why I turned it into a product." That post lives forever on Hacker News, Indie Hackers, and dev Twitter. Word of mouth in this community is unusually fast.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** This is far too vertical for Salesforce or HubSpot to care about. The nuances (Steam key logistics, embargo etiquette, how Twitch differs from YouTube coverage timing) are invisible to anyone who hasn't shipped an indie game. A solo dev who plays and follows indie releases can build the exact right mental model into the product without a product manager guessing at it.

**Biggest risk:** Developers convince themselves a $9/month Notion template is close enough. The pitch has to make the ROI of saved time and fewer launch-week mistakes viscerally clear, not just feature-clear.

**Idea 3: PackForge**

**Problem:** Bedroom producers who sell sample packs on Gumroad, their own Shopify, or Splice spend 3–6 tedious hours per release doing pure mechanical work: normalizing audio to a target ceiling, batch-renaming files to commercial conventions ("Cmaj_120bpm_DrumLoop_01.wav"), generating MP3 preview renders for store pages, building the correct folder structure, writing BPM and key metadata into file headers, and packaging the final ZIP. Every step is manual. They have Audacity open, a Finder window, and a text editor, and they're making errors. This is 100% automatable, and no tool exists that does the whole chain in one pass.

**Target buyer:** Bedroom producers and lo-fi/hip-hop beatmakers releasing 2–6 sample packs per year and making $500–$5K per pack — specifically people already selling, not aspiring to sell. They have an established workflow they hate and money coming in that justifies buying a tool that saves them a half-day per release.

**Monetization:** One-time desktop purchase at $59. No subscription — this is a utility, not a platform. 1,000 sales = $59K. The beauty is that sample pack creators sell on Gumroad — you can list PackForge on Gumroad and be in the same store your buyers already trust. A "lite" tier that processes 20 files free drives trial conversions.

**MVP scope:** Desktop app (Python + PyQt or Tauri). Drag a folder of WAV files onto the app. Set a normalization target (-6dBFS default, adjustable). Configure a rename template with variables the user fills in (KEY, BPM, TYPE, INDEX). Batch rename and normalize in one pass. Render MP3 previews at 192kbps. Output into a structured release folder. ZIP it. Does NOT yet do: BPM or key detection (user inputs these), DAW plugin integration, cloud upload, metadata scraping from file tags, or any streaming platform submission.

**Distribution:** r/WeAreTheMusicMakers, r/samplesforall, Looperman forums, producer-focused YouTube tutorials. The meta-distribution play: list it on Gumroad, where sample pack sellers already browse. A YouTube video called "How I prep and package sample packs for release in under 10 minutes" is both marketing and a tutorial — that format gets shared in producer Discord servers constantly.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** No startup will look at "tool for people who make sample packs" and see a business. The TAM is invisible on a spreadsheet but perfectly real to the 10,000+ producers selling packs. A solo dev who makes music understands exactly which part of this workflow is the most painful (it's the renaming, always the renaming) and can build around that without a committee.

**Biggest risk:** Someone posts a free Python script on GitHub that does 70% of this, the community adopts it, and the ceiling for a paid version collapses. The defense is polish, UX, and a Windows/Mac installer — most producers won't run a script from a terminal.

**Idea 4: Catterie**

**Problem:** Small-scale registered cat breeders (TICA/CFA, 2–6 breeding cats, 10–40 kittens per year) run their operation across paper pedigree binders, Excel spreadsheets for litter tracking, email threads for buyer waitlists, and sticky notes for vaccination schedules. They need to generate pedigree certificates for buyers, match kittens to waitlisted buyers by color and pattern preference, track which cats have been tested for HCM or PKD, log vet visits per cat, and follow up with buyers post-placement for show results. Nothing connects these workflows. Generic pet apps don't understand pedigrees. Livestock apps (Breedr, Herdwatch) are built for cattle. The breeders are running a small business with 1990s tools.

**Target buyer:** TICA- or CFA-registered small catteries in the US selling kittens at $1,500–$4,000 each — breeders doing this as a serious hobby-business, not a commercial mill. Specifically Maine Coon, Ragdoll, Bengal, and Scottish Fold breeders, where demand and prices are highest and breeders are most organized online.

**Monetization:** SaaS at $19/month or $149/year. 500 breeders = $74,500 ARR. Cat breeders are already paying for TICA registration fees, genetic testing ($50–$150 per test), and health certifications — software at $19/month is trivial compared to their existing costs.

**MVP scope:** Web app (React + Python/FastAPI). Cat profiles with parent and grandparent links (pedigree tree). Litter records (date, sire/dam, kitten count, colors, individual kitten notes). Buyer waitlist with color/pattern preferences and contact info. Kitten-to-buyer assignment flow. Vaccination and vet visit log per cat. Printable pedigree certificate (3-generation PDF). Does NOT yet do: automated TICA registration filing, coefficient of inbreeding calculation, kitten contract template generation, photo management, or financial tracking.

**Distribution:** TICA and CFA breeder forums. Facebook groups for specific breeds are enormous and extremely active — Maine Coon breeders alone have multiple groups with 10K+ members who trust recommendations from other breeders. One respected breeder posting "I've been using this for 3 months and it replaced my three spreadsheets" is worth 50 paid ads. Reach out directly to 20 breeders for beta access before launch.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** The niche is too small and specific for enterprise software. The breeders are organized, opinionated, and actively looking for solutions — they just don't expect a developer to show up. A developer who owns cats can participate in the community legitimately, which matters enormously in a trust-based hobby network.

**Biggest risk:** The breeder demographic skews toward people who have managed with binders for 20 years and see software as friction rather than relief. Onboarding is the hard problem — getting their existing records into the system is the moment they quit.

**Idea 5: Stellar Canon**

**Problem:** Hard sci-fi writers and indie tabletop RPG designers building multi-book series or detailed game settings need scientific consistency across their world bible. They establish that a colony is 4.2 light-years away, that their FTL drive covers 1 LY per week, that a character left Earth in 2341 — and then three books later they write a travel scene that contradicts all three of those numbers without noticing. Generic worldbuilding tools (World Anvil, Campfire, Notion) store facts but don't know physics. They can't tell you that if your star is K-type and your planet orbits at 0.7 AU, its year is roughly 240 local days — or flag that your stated travel time between two systems violates the FTL rules you established in chapter two. Writers either do this math manually with Wolfram Alpha open, or quietly hope readers don't catch it. Readers always catch it.

**Target buyer:** Indie sci-fi novelists self-publishing series on KDP (especially those writing military sci-fi, space opera, or hard sci-fi where reader expectations for consistency are highest), and tabletop RPG designers publishing on DriveThruRPG or Backerkit — specifically people mid-series with an existing canon they're actively trying to keep consistent, not worldbuilders who never finish.

**Monetization:** SaaS at $12/month or $89/year. A $149 lifetime license on launch for early adopters. Target: 400 paying users = $35,600 ARR. Sci-fi writers are already paying for Scrivener, World Anvil Pro, and Campfire — a focused tool that solves a specific consistency problem slots into an established "writer buys tools" behavior pattern.

**MVP scope:** Web app. Star system builder: input star spectral type → get estimated habitable zone range, luminosity class. Planet configurator: input orbital distance and planet mass → output surface gravity (in g), orbital period (in Earth days and local days if you set day length), rough temperature estimate. FTL rules engine: define your drive (speed in light-years per day/week/month) and the app calculates travel time between any two defined locations — and flags if you log a scene where the travel time stated in prose doesn't match the math. Timeline tracker with conversion between Earth calendar and local planet calendar. Does NOT yet do: story text editing, AI writing assistance, character relationship graphs, atmospheric chemistry modeling, or export to Scrivener/World Anvil formats.

**Distribution:** r/worldbuilding, r/scifiwriting, r/HFY (huge community of space military sci-fi writers). NaNoWriMo forums in November. Build a free "design your star system" single-page tool with shareable output — that goes viral among sci-fi fans on social media and drives top-of-funnel. Guest post on hard sci-fi author blogs ("The physics checklist I use before every book goes to my editor").

**Why a solo dev wins here:** World Anvil is a platform requiring a team to maintain. This is a sharp, single-purpose tool that does one thing no existing product does: enforces physics consistency. A solo dev who reads hard sci-fi and has felt the annoyance of contradictions in a favorite series is the exact right person to define what "correct" means here — no amount of user research replaces having the problem yourself.

**Biggest risk:** The Venn diagram of "cares enough about scientific consistency to pay for a tool" and "actually finishes and publishes work" may be smaller than the much larger group who worldbuilds endlessly without shipping. Validate by targeting published authors first, not aspirational ones.
