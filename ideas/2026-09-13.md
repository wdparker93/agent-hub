# Weekly App Ideas — September 13, 2026

**Idea 1: HandloadHQ**

**Problem:** Precision rifle shooters and pistol competitors who reload their own ammunition ("handloaders") are running their entire load development process on Excel spreadsheets or inside paper notebooks. They need to correlate variables — powder charge weight, primer brand, bullet seating depth, case brand — against measured outcomes like group size, muzzle velocity from a chronograph, and standard deviation. QuickLOAD ($150) predicts ballistics but doesn't journal real-world results. The few mobile apps in this space are basically glorified recipe cards with no analytics layer. When a handloader finds a node that shoots ¼ MOA, they need to be able to reproduce it exactly and understand *why* it worked. The data is there; the tooling to surface patterns across sessions is not.

**Target buyer:** Precision rifle competitors (PRS, NRL, F-Class) and serious long-range hobby shooters who reload for accuracy, not just cost savings — people spending $300-800/year on brass, powder, and primers and treating load development as a scientific process.

**Monetization:** One-time desktop license at $45. Optional $6/month cloud sync tier for backup and multi-machine access. At 1,000 licenses sold — a very reachable ceiling for a niche this passionate — that's $45K revenue. Ongoing cloud ARR layers on top. No per-unit cost, no App Store cut if distributed direct.

**MVP scope:** A desktop app (Tauri + React or Electron) that lets users define firearm profiles, create load recipes, log shooting sessions with environmental data (temp, altitude, wind), enter velocity strings from a chronograph, and attach target photos or group measurements. Auto-calculates SD/ES from velocity strings. Shows a scatter plot of charge weight vs. group size across sessions so you can visually find your pressure node. Does NOT do ballistic prediction, does NOT integrate with smart targets or Bluetooth chronographs yet.

**Distribution:** r/reloading (280K members), Sniper's Hide forum (the definitive long-range precision community), Precision Rifle Blog, sponsoring a segment on a reloading-focused YouTube channel. Offer a lifetime license at $29 to the first 100 buyers via those communities to get real feedback fast.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** This is too small and too legally careful for large companies to touch (anything adjacent to firearms makes corporate lawyers nervous), and the incumbent QuickLOAD hasn't updated its UI since roughly 2003. The precision shooting community is underserved by software and will adopt tools that actually respect their workflow. A solo dev who can talk the language of MOA and ballistic coefficient earns trust immediately.

**Biggest risk:** The market is passionate but might be smaller than it feels from inside the hobby. Validate by posting a waitlist landing page on Sniper's Hide before writing a line of code.

**Idea 2: SampleSheriff**

**Problem:** Bedroom producers who've been buying sample packs for a few years are sitting on libraries of 20,000–100,000 samples spread across inconsistently named folders — "SP_Drums_Vol3," "FREE PACK [DO NOT DISTRIBUTE]," "808s final FINAL." DAWs have terrible built-in browsers: no BPM detection on search, no key tagging, no cross-folder search. Splice solves this only for Splice content. Soundminer is $200 and aimed at sound designers in post-production. The result: producers re-buy sounds they already own and spend 40 minutes hunting for the one hi-hat they remember hearing six months ago.

**Target buyer:** Bedroom producers who release on Bandcamp or SoundCloud, buy packs from Splice, Loopmasters, or Gumroad, and work in FL Studio, Ableton, or Logic — specifically the ones who have 10+ gigabytes of samples they can't effectively search.

**Monetization:** $29 one-time for the desktop app. Add a $5/month tier for metadata cloud backup and cross-machine sync (so your tags survive a drive wipe). 2,000 sales in year one = $58K. Realistic given how many producers exist and how vocal this pain is.

**MVP scope:** A Python/Electron desktop app that indexes a directory of samples — runs BPM detection via librosa, key detection via librosa's chromagram, and auto-classifies file type (kick, snare, loop, one-shot) by filename heuristics and spectral features. Outputs a searchable local database with an in-app browser and waveform preview player. Lets users add custom tags. Does NOT sync with any DAW directly, does NOT have AI "find similar sounds" yet, does NOT touch cloud storage.

**Distribution:** r/edmproduction, r/hiphopheads production threads, Gumroad launch (their audience skews producer-heavy), cold DMs to mid-size producer YouTube channels for affiliate deals. A 60-second demo video of searching "130bpm minor loop" and instantly getting results will spread organically in production communities.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** Big DAW companies have no incentive to build a tool that works across competitors' software. Splice solves only the Splice problem. A fast, local-first, privacy-respecting tool that works on *your* files — not their cloud — is exactly what the privacy-conscious part of this community wants.

**Biggest risk:** Audio analysis (especially key detection) is imprecise enough to erode trust. Nail the UI for *correcting* bad auto-tags easily, or users give up after seeing wrong keys.

**Idea 3: PressRun**

**Problem:** A solo indie developer finishes their Steam game and needs to get coverage. They collect email addresses for journalists, streamers, and YouTubers, generate Steam keys, paste them into individual emails, and then have no idea what happened — did the key get used? Did anyone cover it? Should they follow up at the two-week mark or is that annoying? Keymailer exists but is built for publishers, not solo devs, and it's opaque about what coverage it actually delivers. presskit() was the community standard for years and is now an abandoned HTML template. There is no tool that combines: a professional-looking press kit page + key distribution + coverage tracking in one place designed for a one-person studio.

**Target buyer:** Solo indie developers launching on Steam or itch.io with a budget of zero for a PR firm — people who taught themselves Unity or Godot, made a game, and are figuring out marketing for the first time.

**Monetization:** $19/month while actively marketing a game, with a free tier for building the kit before launch. Alternatively, a $49/game flat fee (one-time, no subscription pressure). 300 paying devs at $19/month = $5,700 MRR. The market is large — thousands of games launch on Steam annually.

**MVP scope:** A web SaaS (React + Python backend) where devs create a hosted press kit page (description, screenshots, trailer embed, factsheet, contact form), upload a batch of Steam or itch.io keys, invite press contacts from a list or link, track who clicked the kit, who redeemed a key, and manually log coverage when it appears. Does NOT auto-crawl the web for coverage, does NOT auto-generate press lists, does NOT handle NDA gating of assets.

**Distribution:** r/gamedev and r/indiegaming, the GameDev.tv and Godot Discord servers, #devlog threads on Twitter/X where solo devs are already venting about launch anxiety. Writing a genuinely useful blog post — "How I got 40 reviews for my $8 Steam game without a PR budget" — and linking PressRun naturally drives inbound.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** The problem is acute only at small scale. A solo dev building this *is* the customer and can design with intuitions a corporate product team would never have. The tool's value prop is essentially: "I know how scared you are on launch day, here's the checklist that doesn't suck."

**Biggest risk:** Devs are cheap. The free tier has to be valuable enough to attract users but the paid trigger (key tracking analytics) has to be genuinely necessary, not easily worked around with a spreadsheet.

**Idea 4: PixelPrice**

**Problem:** Retro game collectors track collection value in three places: VGPC (Video Game Price Charting) for market data, a separate spreadsheet for their owned games, and their brain for their wishlist. VGPC is a data resource, not a portfolio tool — it has no personal layer, no collection value over time, no price alerts when a specific item you want dips below a threshold on eBay. CollectR on mobile is basic and mobile-only. The collector who owns 400 SNES carts and wants to know "what is my collection worth today vs. six months ago" and "alert me when a complete-in-box copy of Earthbound hits below $180" has no dedicated tool for that workflow.

**Target buyer:** Serious retro collectors (SNES, N64, Sega Saturn, PS1/PS2 era) who spend $50–300/month on games, track eBay sold listings compulsively, and have opinions about what qualifies as "loose," "CIB," and "NIB" pricing.

**Monetization:** $6/month subscription. 500 subscribers = $3,000 MRR — small but achievable in a hobby where people casually spend $150 on a single cartridge. Annual plan at $55 for upfront cash flow.

**MVP scope:** A browser extension (Chrome/Firefox) that detects when you're on a VGPC or eBay listing page and offers a one-click "Add to Collection" or "Add to Wishlist" button. A companion web dashboard shows your collection's rolling market value (pulled from VGPC's price data), a watchlist with target price inputs, and email alerts when eBay sold prices for a wishlist item cross below your threshold. Does NOT support automatic eBay purchasing, does NOT cover every platform on day one (start with SNES + N64 + Genesis).

**Distribution:** r/GameCollecting (800K members), r/retrogaming, the Digital Press and Nintendo Age forums, and cold outreach to YouTube channels in the "game hunting" subgenre (Slope's Game Room, Metal Jesus Rocks type audience). A video of "I set a price alert and bought CIB Link's Awakening DX for $12 under market" is a ready-made organic hook.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** VGPC is run by a small team focused on being the price database, not a portfolio product. eBay has no interest in helping collectors track value across sellers. The niche is well-defined, the users are online and opinionated, and the data API access is manageable at small scale.

**Biggest risk:** VGPC rate-limiting or terms-of-service changes that break the data layer. Mitigate by building direct eBay completed-listing scraping as the backup data source from day one.

**Idea 5: GlucoseLog for Cats**

**Problem:** Diabetic cats require twice-daily blood glucose testing, insulin dosing, and careful logging — and there are a shocking number of them (feline diabetes affects roughly 1 in 200 cats). The community managing this is large, active, and technically sophisticated. They currently run their cats' glucose curves in Google Sheets or in a Facebook group spreadsheet template pinned by volunteers. The vet wants a report at every visit. The owner wants to know: is this dose working, is the curve flattening, when did we last have a hypoglycemic episode? No app exists that models the feline glucose curve specifically (it differs from canine and human patterns), generates vet-printable reports, or alerts when a reading is dangerously low.

**Target buyer:** Cat owners managing feline diabetes — people already spending $100–300/month on insulin, syringes, glucose meters, and prescription food, who are logging data every day and desperately want tooling that isn't a spreadsheet.

**Monetization:** $8/month subscription or $69/year. This buyer is already paying for their cat's life; $8/month is trivially below the cost of one insulin vial. 400 subscribers = $3,200 MRR. The community is global and concentrated in English-language forums.

**MVP scope:** A web app (React + Python) where users define a cat profile, log glucose readings with timestamp and pre/post-meal flag, log insulin doses, and see a glucose curve chart for any date range. Exports a clean PDF report formatted for vet visits. Sends a push/email alert if a logged reading is below a user-set hypoglycemia threshold. Does NOT integrate with glucometers via Bluetooth yet, does NOT support canine or human diabetes.

**Distribution:** The Feline Diabetes Message Board (FDMB) — this is the definitive community, thousands of active members, and they will review and recommend tools vigorously if the tool is good. Also r/felinediabetes, the Facebook group "Diabetic Cat Care," and cold outreach to feline internal medicine vets who might recommend it to newly diagnosed patients.

**Why a solo dev wins here:** No pharmaceutical company will build this (liability), no big pet app company cares about a condition affecting a fraction of a percent of cats, and the community is too small for a VC-backed startup. It's exactly the right size for a solo dev. The FDMB community will beta test it honestly, spread it organically, and tell you exactly what to build next — they've been waiting for someone who shows up.

**Biggest risk:** Regulatory creep — if you add any language that sounds like medical dosing advice rather than a logging tool, you're in murky FDA territory around veterinary software. Keep the product framed as a data journal and reporting tool, never a dosing calculator.
